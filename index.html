Abstract

We present a GPU-native volumetric-video system for real-time reconstruction of graphics-compatible textured triangle meshes from multiple RGB-D sensors. Existing TSDF systems accelerate fusion and mesh extraction, but extending them to per-frame textured meshing adds CPU mesh processing, CPU-side texturing work, or extra CPU–GPU transfers between stages. After input staging, our method keeps the reconstruction and texturing stages (TSDF fusion, Marching Cubes, connected-component noise removal, view-based texture mapping, inpainting, and radiometric seam correction) resident on the GPU; no intermediate mesh or image data returns to the host between these stages. A fixed-size mesh-topology representation gives constant-time neighbor access on the GPU and makes the downstream mesh-level operations data-parallel. Because we texture each face from a single camera, exposure seams that vary smoothly across each image remain; we estimate a per-camera low-frequency gain field and bake it into the atlas on the GPU. On the CMU Panoptic dataset, our method is faster than the baselines at every tested voxel resolution and number of sensors, achieves peak speedups of more than an order of magnitude over CUDA-accelerated nvblox and CPU-based Open3D, and approaches an offline textured-meshing baseline in perceptual re-rendering quality.

Method

GPU-native textured meshing

Depth from all sensors is fused in a single TSDF kernel, meshed with Marching Cubes, and cleaned by a GPU connected-component pass. Each face takes its color from the best camera; faces no camera sees are filled by propagating views from their neighbors. The input color images are stacked into one texture atlas, so the mesh UVs are simply pixel coordinates in the source images.

Seam-aware radiometric correction

Where neighboring faces use different cameras, exposure differences show up as seams. We sample both cameras along every seam, solve a smooth 24×16 log-gain field per camera with a few matrix-free Jacobi iterations, and bake it into the atlas — all on the GPU, adding a few milliseconds per frame. A temporal moving average keeps the correction stable in video.
